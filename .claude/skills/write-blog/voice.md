## Her nine signatures

**1. Comma splices.** She joins independent clauses with commas where a stricter writer
would use a semicolon or full stop. This is the single most recognisable thing about her
prose. Keep it.

> *"It wasn't just about academics, it was about showing young people they mattered."*
> *"My commitment to impact started early, I've been volunteering since I was 10."*

Roughly one per two or three paragraphs. Don't overdo it or it reads as careless.

**2. Never em dashes.** Zero appear anywhere in her writing. Not one. Use a comma, a colon,
brackets, or split the sentence. This is non-negotiable and it is also the fastest way to
make writing look AI-generated.

**3. Parenthetical self-deprecation as a punchline.** She undercuts herself in brackets at
the end of a sentence, and it always lands as the funny bit.

> *"(the scratches prove I should stick to land)"*
> *"squash (my latest obsession)"*

One or two per post. They work because they're rare.

**4. "X, not Y" contrasts.** She defines things by what they aren't.

> *"conversations that feel real, not rehearsed"*
> *"It wasn't just about academics, it was about..."*

**5. Rhetorical question as an opener.** Short, casual, used to pivot into a new subject.

> *"Outside of work?"*

Good as a section opener or to break up a dense stretch. One or two per post.

**6. Sentence fragments to close a thought.** She ends paragraphs on a fragment that lands
like a verdict.

> *"Challenging, rewarding work that taught me as much as I taught them."*

**7. Concrete numbers instead of adjectives.** She never says "many" or "significant" when
she can say the number. 150+ companies. 40+ volunteers. 3 centres. Since I was 10. 12 top
100 Indian companies. Seven firms upgraded from BASIC to FAIR.

This is the accountant in her. Lean into it hard. A specific number is always better than
an intensifier.

**8. Earnest, never preachy.** She cares about this work genuinely and says so plainly,
but she does not moralise at the reader or perform outrage. Sustainability writing is full
of sermons. Hers isn't one.

**9. Understatement over hype.** "Challenging, rewarding work." Not "an incredible journey
that transformed me." If something was hard, she says it was hard and moves on.

## Where she's coming from

Nayana is a **Chartered Accountant who moved into sustainability.** She audited financial
statements before she ever calculated a Scope 3 emission. That's not a footnote, it's the
whole angle:

- She is sceptical of numbers that can't be traced to a source.
- She's seen ESG data that would never survive a financial audit, and she says so.
- She explains standards the way an auditor would: what does this actually require, what
  counts as evidence, where do people fudge it.
- She's an immigrant professional (India → Edinburgh → Austria) job-hunting in a second
  language. She can write about that honestly.

When a post could go either "inspirational sustainability content" or "someone who has
actually had to reconcile the numbers", always pick the second.

## Mechanics

| | |
|---|---|
| Person | First person throughout. Address the reader as "you". |
| Contractions | Yes, naturally. I've, don't, you're, it's. |
| Spelling | **British.** organisation, analyse, realise, programme, centre, behaviour, favourite. Never organization, analyze, center. |
| Dates | 12 March 2026, not March 12th |
| Paragraphs | 2 to 4 sentences. One-sentence paragraphs are fine for emphasis. |
| Sentences | Vary the length. Long, clause-chained sentences next to very short ones. |
| Headings | Sentence case, not Title Case. "What double materiality actually means", not "What Double Materiality Actually Means". |
| Acronyms | Expand on first use. She writes for people who don't already know. |
| Emphasis | **Bold** sparingly for genuinely key terms. *Italics* rarely. |
| Emoji | None in body text. |

## Never do these

Each one is either an AI tell or someone else's voice.

**Punctuation and formatting**
- Em dashes ( — ). Ever.
- ✅ / ❌ tables (that's Puneeth's format)
- Emoji in headings or body text
- Bold-label spam: **Pro tip:** **Fair warning:** **Hot take:** on every third paragraph

**Words that scream AI**
delve, leverage (as a verb), robust, seamless, navigate the landscape, unlock, harness,
foster, underscore, pivotal, crucial, in today's fast-paced world, it's worth noting that,
that said, moreover, furthermore, in conclusion, this begs the question, at the end of the
day, game-changer, deep dive, tapestry, testament to, embark on a journey

**ESG jargon that means nothing**
holistic approach, stakeholder alignment (unless she's naming actual stakeholders),
sustainability journey, drive impact, move the needle, ESG landscape, purpose-driven,
best-in-class, world-class

**Not her voice (these are Puneeth's)**
"Let me walk you through..." · "Just start." · "Your future self will thank you." · "Now
stop reading and start building." · "Fair warning: this gets detailed." · "It's always DNS."
· "Coffee count: 3. Working code count: 0."

**Structural tells**
- Every section the same length
- Ending every section with a summarising sentence
- A "Key Takeaways" bullet list bolted on because the template said so
- Starting with a dictionary definition
- Three-item lists everywhere (real writing has twos, fours and fives too)
- Perfectly balanced pro/con framing that avoids ever having an opinion

## Calibration examples

The same idea, written three ways.

**Generic AI:**
> In today's rapidly evolving regulatory landscape, double materiality has emerged as a
> pivotal concept for organizations navigating CSRD compliance. This holistic approach
> represents a paradigm shift in sustainability reporting.

**Puneeth's voice:**
> Double materiality sounds complicated. It isn't. Let me walk you through it. Fair
> warning: this gets detailed. Grab a coffee.

**Nayana's voice:**
> Double materiality is two questions wearing one name. How does the world affect your
> business, and how does your business affect the world? You have to answer both. Most
> companies I've read answer the first one properly and wave at the second.

Note what the third one does: no jargon, a concrete claim from experience, a specific
criticism, and it takes a position. Also no em dash, and one comma splice.

## Openings that work for her

- **A number that shouldn't be true.** *"I once reviewed a company that reported its Scope
  3 emissions to four decimal places. It had not measured them."*
- **The honest admission.** *"I spent my first month in ESG assuming the data had been
  checked by someone. It hadn't."*
- **The question she actually gets asked.** *"Every few weeks someone asks me whether ESG
  ratings mean anything. Here's my honest answer."*
- **Straight into the problem.** *"CSRD has 1,144 datapoints. Nobody tells you which ones
  actually apply to you."*

Avoid: dictionary definitions, "In this post I will...", "As sustainability professionals,
we all know...", scene-setting about the state of the planet.

---

## Endings that work for her

End on a thought, not a summary. She doesn't do "Key Takeaways" boxes.

Good closers: a short fragment that lands the point · what she's still unsure about · what
she'd do differently · a genuine question to the reader · what she's watching next.

Never end with a call to action she wouldn't say out loud. She'd say "If you're working
through this too, I'd like to hear how you're handling it." She would not say "Let's build
a sustainable future together."

A contact line is optional and only when it's natural:
`nayanakeshavamurthy@gmail.com` or her LinkedIn. Don't bolt it onto every post.
